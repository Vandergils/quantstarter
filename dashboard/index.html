What is a backtest?

A backtest replays old prices and asks: "What would have happened if I had followed this rule?" The rule might be "buy when a fast average rises above a slow one". It is a way to check an idea cheaply before risking money. It is not a prediction, because markets change and the future rarely repeats the past.

Reading the equity curve

The green line shows how 1.00 invested at the start would have grown or shrunk. The dashed grey line is buy and hold, which means buying once and doing nothing. It is your benchmark. A strategy that cannot beat simply holding, or at least be much safer, is hard to justify, because holding is free and effortless.

Fees add up fast

Every buy and every sell costs a fee, and real orders also lose a little to slippage, because you rarely trade at exactly the price you saw. A strategy that trades a lot pays both again and again. Try it:

Approximate share of your account lost to fees alone, before any price move.

The Cost stress test button runs your strategy again at rising costs per trade and tells you at what cost the profit disappears. A strategy that only works at tiny costs is fragile.

Drawdown: how deep is the hole?

Max drawdown is the worst fall from a peak to a later low. It shows what you would have had to live through. A big drawdown is hard to sit through, and many people sell at the bottom. Losses are also harder to recover than they look:

Sharpe ratio

Sharpe measures return per unit of risk, so a smooth +10% scores higher than a wild +10%. As a rough guide: below 0 means it lost money, 0 to 1 is weak, and above 1 is decent. Treat very high values (like 3 or more) with suspicion, since they are more often a bug or overfitting than a discovery. Sharpe is scaled to a year using the timeframe of the data you chose, so it is not comparable between hourly and daily data.

Overfitting: the biggest trap

If you test hundreds of settings and keep the best one, you have mostly found the setting that fit the noise in old data. It looks brilliant on the past and often fails on new prices. Four tools in this dashboard fight it:

  • Unseen test period. The newest part of the data is held back and never used to choose settings.
  • Find best settings. Picks a winner on the training part, then shows the unseen result. A big drop is overfitting in action.
  • Walk-forward test. Repeats this over several time windows. A good idea should win in most of them, and its best setting should not change every time.
  • Settings map. Tests a whole grid of neighbouring settings. A real edge shows as a broad green region, and overfitting shows as one bright cell surrounded by red.

Reading the settings map

Pick two settings (for example the fast and slow average) and press Draw settings map. You get two colour grids: one scored on the training data and one on the unseen data. Every cell is one combination of settings.

  • A broad green area on both grids means the idea does not depend on exact numbers. That is a good sign.
  • One bright cell surrounded by red is a lucky spike. Picking it means picking the luck.
  • Green on the left grid, red on the right is overfitting: settings that fit the past and fail on new prices.
  • Mostly red on both means the strategy itself does not work on this data, whatever settings you choose.

Does it work on other coins?

An idea that worked on one coin may just have been lucky there. Compare across assets runs the same strategy, with the same settings, on every coin in your database and shows the unseen result for each next to buy and hold. If it only works on one coin, be suspicious. Keep in mind that crypto coins tend to move together, so five coins are not five independent tests.

To add coins, run python collector/collect_yahoo.py ETH-USD SOL-USD and reload the page.

Hourly, 4-hour or daily candles?

The Data menu offers every timeframe you have collected. Daily candles from Yahoo go back many years, which means many more trades, more market phases (bull, bear, sideways) and a much more trustworthy test than two years of hourly data. Hourly candles show fast behaviour but cover a short span, and a short span is how a lucky result happens.

  • Settings count candles. A slow average of 50 means 50 hours on hourly data and 50 days on daily data, so the same setting is a very different strategy.
  • Results are not comparable across timeframes, because each has its own number of candles per year in Sharpe and a different cost per trade impact.
  • To download them: python collector/collect_yahoo.py BTC-USD --timeframe 1d

Going short

A short is a bet that the price will fall: you borrow the coin, sell it, and buy it back later, hopefully cheaper. In Long and short mode the strategy holds the coin when it is bullish and is short when it is bearish, so it can profit in a falling market, which a long-only strategy cannot.

  • It is always in the market, so the swings are bigger, and a wrong call hurts in both directions.
  • A short can lose more than you put in. A long can fall to zero, but a price can rise without limit.
  • Shorts cost money to hold. Futures charge funding and margin loans charge interest. Use the funding slider, because zero flatters short results.
  • Flipping costs two trades: closing the long and opening the short.
  • Not modelled: liquidation when the price moves against you, margin limits, borrow availability, and funding rates that change over time. Real results can be worse than the backtest.

Mistakes that fake good results

  • Look-ahead bias. Using information the strategy could not have had yet. Here every trade happens on the next candle to prevent it.
  • Ignoring fees. Many trades can turn a winner into a loser.
  • Too few trades. With 10 trades, luck can explain everything.
  • One asset, one period. A rising market makes almost any "buy" rule look smart.
  • Skipping reality. Real trading also has slippage, delays and exchange outages that a simple backtest ignores.

Risk per trade

Even a good strategy can have a losing streak. A common beginner rule is to risk only about 1% of the account on any single trade. The position size calculator on the Backtest tab works out how much to buy so that your stop-loss costs only that percentage.

Checklist before trusting a result

  • It held up on the unseen test, not just the training part
  • It beat buy and hold, or was clearly safer (smaller drawdown)
  • It won in most walk-forward windows
  • It still works with a higher cost (see the Cost stress test)
  • Nearby settings give similar results on the settings map, not just one lucky value
  • It made enough trades for the result to mean something
  • It also holds up on other coins (Compare across assets)
  • If it goes short, the result still holds with a realistic funding cost

Even when every box is ticked, a backtest only says "worth testing further", never "will make money". This is educational, not financial advice.
